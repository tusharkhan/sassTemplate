Cutting glass-to-glass latency from 210ms to 47ms

We spent a quarter tearing apart our WebRTC pipeline and found most of the delay was in places nobody was measuring. Here is every change we shipped, in the order the numbers improved.

RN

Rina Nakamura

Principal Engineer, Media Pipeline

11 August 2026 · 12 min read

When we published our first latency number in 2024 it was 210 milliseconds, measured from the moment light hit a camera sensor to the moment the matching frame lit up a viewer’s screen. That was competitive at the time. It was also, we discovered, mostly buffer.

Measure the whole path, not your own service

Our dashboards tracked the time a frame spent inside our SFU. That number was excellent — a median of 9ms — and it told us almost nothing, because it excluded capture, encode, the first network hop, the jitter buffer and the decoder. The first real fix was instrumentation: a synthetic publisher in every region that stamps frames on capture and a synthetic subscriber that reads the stamp back off the decoded surface.

Once we could see the whole path, the breakdown was uncomfortable. Only 12% of the delay was ours in the sense we had been measuring. The jitter buffer alone accounted for 78ms.

Where the 163ms went

  • Jitter buffer, 78ms. Sized for the worst network we had ever seen rather than the network in front of us.
  • Encoder lookahead, 41ms. A quality setting inherited from our VOD transcoder, where two frames of lookahead are free.
  • Forwarding hops, 29ms. Traffic crossed two internal regions before reaching the viewer’s edge.
  • Render scheduling, 15ms. Frames waiting on the next compositor tick in the browser.

Make the jitter buffer adaptive, then honest

A jitter buffer trades latency for smoothness, and a fixed one makes that trade on behalf of every connection at once. We replaced ours with a controller that tracks the 99th percentile of inter-arrival delay over a sliding ten-second window and targets one frame of headroom above it.

jitter-controller.js

// Target one frame of headroom above observed p99 jitter.
const target = Math.max(
  MIN_BUFFER_MS,
  jitter.percentile(99) + 1000 / stream.fps
);

if (Math.abs(buffer.depthMs - target) > 8) {
  buffer.resize(target, { mode: 'glide' });
}

The glide mode matters more than the sizing. Resizing a buffer abruptly either drops a frame or stalls one, both of which viewers notice far more than 30ms of extra delay. Gliding spreads the change across roughly forty frames by nudging playback rate by less than half a percent, which is inaudible and invisible.

Every millisecond we removed came from a default that was correct for a different product. None of it came from writing faster code.

Results

Across four releases the median landed at 47ms, with the 95th percentile at 91ms. Rebuffer ratio — the metric we actually care about — improved by 0.4 percentage points rather than regressing, because the adaptive buffer is larger than the old fixed one on genuinely bad connections.
